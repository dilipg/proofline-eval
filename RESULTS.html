Four Branches on arXiv

The scorer-evaluation brief was written against a synthetic corpus with planted ground truth. This is what happens when all four of its branches are run against 60,000 real arXiv cards instead: where nothing is planted, the citation graph is genuinely incomplete, and nearly half the record is superseded.

What this revision changed

Making the harness answer every query as of its own date moved one conclusion: the cross-encoder, not the metadata filter, is the largest single retrieval win. The first version's filter was reading the future.
  • Supersession is now judged as of the query's date. hybrid_rrf_fresh and rerank used to drop every card that had been superseded by the end of the record. A query dated 2012 therefore lost every paper revised in 2015, which is knowledge nobody had in 2012, and it correlates with not being cited: the old rule removed 11% of each query's candidates but only 5.1% of its relevant ones. Filtering by what was current on the query's date makes every relevant version reachable (100%, up from 94.9%) and costs hybrid_rrf_fresh 0.006 nDCG@10 (95% CI 0.004 to 0.008) against the old rule, paired on the same queries and labels.
  • A citation points at the version that existed when it was made. 9,665 citations (1.1%) resolved to a version written after the citing paper; they are now dropped rather than pointed at the future.
  • The sample is reproducible. The snowball sampler walked sets of arXiv ids, whose order Python salts per process, so the same data and seed drew a different corpus every run. The first version's figures were one such draw. Every figure below is from one fixed sample, and each fix was measured on it twice, with the code before and after, so what the fixes moved is separable from what the sample moved.

What was measured

158,160 arXiv papers were joined to the ogbn-arxiv citation graph, then a connected subgraph was grown by snowball sampling: random sampling would have kept only ~(2.5/158)² of the edges and destroyed the graph that makes this corpus worth using. Every paper version becomes its own card, chained by supersedes_id, so supersession is real rather than synthesised.

Embeddings are SPECTER2 (768-dim), which is asymmetric: documents encode through the proximity adapter, queries through adhoc_query. Four candidates were compared against a bm25 baseline. The 30-year span is not incidental: a query dated 1998 is answered against a corpus holding nothing after 1998, so the brief's argument that a frozen test set goes wrong rather than merely stale has three decades of room to bite.

A

Gold from nowhere

Only dag_mined picks the winner both judges picked. The other two sources would have blocked a shipped improvement.

With no planted truth and no humans, the anchor is a calibrated judge scoring the two rankings directly on realistic queries (paper titles) with no labels at all, which is what makes it independent of the sources it grades.

dag_mined and usage_mined differ by 0.299 nDCG on the same corpus, the same two scorers, the same afternoon, and land on opposite sides of the ship/block line. Both are free, both derive from data the product already holds, and both look entirely respectable in isolation. That is the brief's thesis: label quality and label usefulness are different properties, demonstrated rather than argued.

Hold the size loosely and the direction firmly. The first version's sample read 0.218; this one reads 0.299, and 0.308 with the code from before the fixes. The verdicts did not move in any of the three.

The direction is predictable in hindsight. usage_mined labels are built from what the incumbent surfaced in its own top 3, so a scorer that finds something the incumbent never showed is punished by construction. The brief calls this the source to be genuinely afraid of. It is off by 0.299.

The best label source also disqualifies a whole class of scorer

On this corpus dag_mined relevance is the citation graph, and graph_boost scores partly by in-degree in that same graph, so the comparison would score the scorer on its own feature. The harness refuses it in code rather than trusting anyone to remember:

dag_mined   REFUSED: scorer 'graph_boost' reads graph structure and labels 'dag_mined' are derived from it. The comparison would score the scorer on its own feature.

Against the two sources that could score it, graph_boost is catastrophic: −0.781 on generated, −0.675 on usage_mined; in-degree promotes heavily-cited papers, which is precisely wrong when the task is to find one specific paper. It now counts only the citations that existed on the query's date, where it used to count the whole record's; with the whole record's it read −0.650 and −0.604 on this sample, because knowing which papers would be cited later was softening the damage. But that is the side note. The finding is that the only label source which picked the right winner is also the one that disqualifies any scorer reading graph structure, while the brief's own recommended stack includes graph-aware retrieval (§6.10). Those two recommendations collide, and the collision is invisible unless the guard is mechanical.

B

Judge or metric

Two judges agreed with each other at kappa 0.462 , and they are from the same family, so that is an optimistic ceiling.

The more reliable judge is the less useful one. Sonnet 5 is markedly more self-consistent and barely position-biased, and agrees with the ranking metric not at all (κ = −0.007). Haiku is noisier and more biased, yet tracks nDCG better. A judge cannot be selected on reliability alone.

The noise floor, and why the brief needs it

Swap-inconsistency is read throughout the brief as position bias. That only holds for a deterministic judge, and ours is not: removing temperature: 0 to satisfy Sonnet 5, which rejects the parameter outright, left every Anthropic judge sampling: two runs over identical inputs returned κ of +0.121 and +0.197.

So the same pair was judged twice in the same order. Any flip there involves no position change at all:

swap-flip rate = noise + position  →  position = swap − floor

Both judges' swap rates survive the correction: position bias is real and dominant. But roughly a sixth of Haiku's raw 25.3% was the judge disagreeing with itself, a different defect with a different fix (sample twice and take the mode). Without this control the two are indistinguishable.

C

The long record

Degradation is monotonic across a 10× corpus, and the candidates' relative advantage grows as the record gets longer: hybrid_rrf_fresh starts below bm25 at 6,000 cards and ends a third above it at 60,000.

The as-of fix lowered both candidates, most at the small end: paired against the old rule, hybrid_rrf_fresh loses 0.027 at 6,000 and at 20,000 cards and 0.005 at 60,000. At every point the old rule knew which papers would be revised after the query. At the small points it also asked the full record which versions had successors, and a scale point is a smaller record: there, a version whose successor was not sampled is the current one.

The effect is so large that ~17 no-answer queries would suffice to call it. The brief worries you need several hundred and nobody wants to write them; here the subsetting produced them for free: every no-answer query is the title of a real paper held outside the sampled corpus, so its emptiness is verifiable rather than asserted.

D

Run it every day

One candidate clears every hard check and ships. hybrid_rrf is blocked by 2,621 supersession failures, and rerank, the best scorer here, by a single no-answer query it answered.

Nothing came back UNDERPOWERED. At n = 1,484 the minimum detectable effect is 0.005–0.019, and 76 to 159 queries, depending on the candidate, would resolve the 0.02 target. The same harness on the synthetic corpus at n = 219 returned UNDERPOWERED on every single slice. That contrast is the brief's sizing argument made concrete: the test set was the binding constraint, not the scorers.

rerank's block is the gate doing what it says. One of 300 no-answer queries came back answered at its calibrated threshold, and one occurrence blocks: a hard check is never averaged into a score that looks fine. The same query (q_na_dg_000099) fails with the code from before the fixes, so it is this sample's, not the fixes'; the first version's sample happened to hold none.

Two details worth trusting. recently_changed is hybrid_rrf_fresh's strongest slice (+0.043, double its overall), precisely where a freshness filter should pay, so the mechanism is working as designed rather than by accident. And the held-out-proofline slice beats the dev split for all three candidates (+0.041 vs +0.034 for rerank), which is the opposite of the leakage pattern the brief warns about.

Which branch binds

Three branches are downstream of one. The ordering A → D → C → B survives every measurement here, and two of its rungs can now be given numbers instead of arguments.
#BranchWhat this run showed
1A · label sources 0.299 nDCG between two respectable sources, on opposite sides of the ship line
2D · the loop the same gate, on usage_mined, blocks the change that shipped
3C · the long record the candidate-set filter: the BLOCK→SHIP flip, 2,621 failures to zero
4B · judge or metric costliest by far; κ=0.462 between judges, κ=−0.007 for the better one

“Build D as a reporter first” is no longer a caution. The identical Branch D machinery, pointed at usage_mined instead of dag_mined, returns WORSE and blocks hybrid_rrf_fresh, the candidate that ships on every hard check and every slice. Not a hypothetical about automating confident wrong answers on a schedule; a specific release decision inverted by a label-source choice, where both sources look respectable.

Where C sits. Third as an evaluation branch, and its filter is now the smallest of the three retrieval steps, level with fusion, once it is measured without the future. Its single idea, moving supersession out of the ranker and into the candidate set, is still the change that flips the gate: no ranking weight turns 2,621 failures into zero. The first version called it the largest retrieval win as well; that part came from a filter that knew which papers would be revised later.

B last is confirmed on cost as well as value. It consumed real money, three failed runs and several hours of wall clock, and returned two judges agreeing with each other only moderately and the more reliable of them agreeing with the ranking metric not at all. Its one load-bearing use was anchoring Branch A, a service to A rather than a branch in its own right.

What the numbers confirm

The upstream bundle is worth 2.44× the gap between the two scorers under debate, and the cross-encoder is its larger half. The brief's caveat holds, and that is a floor, since field-weighted lexical was never tested.

The brief warns that if the goal is better retrieval rather than better evaluation, the largest win is probably not in any of the four branches: it is a cross-encoder reranker, plus the candidate-set stage, plus field-weighted lexical. Decomposed step by step, that is what the data says.

The first version of this page corrected the brief here: its filter step (+0.0155) edged the cross-encoder (+0.0119), so “reranking is probably the single largest available win” did not hold. That correction was wrong. On the same sample, the filter reads +0.0166 when it drops what was superseded by the end of the record and +0.0106 when it drops only what was superseded by the query's date, while the cross-encoder goes from +0.0111 to +0.0165 on top of it. The brief's claim stands. A paired test on the difference between the two steps was still not run.

Five corrections found by running it

  • “Every branch except B1's label comparison runs unchanged on real data” is false. Six sites outside Branch A hardcoded the planted provenance. With real data they evaluate zero queries and report success rather than failing: the branches are downstream of A in code, not only in argument.
  • Swap-inconsistency is not position bias without a noise control, and the brief treats them as one quantity throughout.
  • The scale test and the query set are coupled. B3 pins every judged document into every scale point; real papers cite ~19 others, so a large query set pins more documents than the smallest point holds and the “6k” point silently becomes 30k. Sampling had to run against a judged-document budget, not a query count: the Branch C trap, one level up from where the brief catches it.
  • “Current” has a date. A supersession filter that asks the end of the record which versions are current scores well and is wrong: it knows which papers will be revised. It must ask as of each query, or the freshness rule becomes a leak from the future.
  • A sample that moves between runs is not a result. The snowball followed Python's salted set order, so the first version's corpus could not be drawn again, and the Branch A gap moved by 0.09 between draws.

What this does not establish

The anchor is a judge, not a human. A judge-anchored Branch A is weaker evidence than a human-anchored one, and the judge grading the label sources has measured biases of its own.

The anchors and Branch B are from the first sample. They were not re-run, since they cost money. The anchors judged hybrid_rrf_fresh over bm25 on the first sample; every label source gives the same verdict on this sample as it did there, so the AGREES / OPPOSITE column is carried over, not re-measured.

Both judges are from one family. The cross-family comparison the brief asks for was blocked by an exhausted daily quota. Two Anthropic models should agree more than two from different vendors, so κ = 0.462 is an optimistic ceiling, which strengthens rather than weakens the conclusion.

Some hard-check zeros are true by construction. hybrid_rrf_fresh and rerank filter to the versions current on the query's date, so they cannot rank a superseded card above its successor. That is the design working as intended, not a ranking achievement. The handicap the first version noted is gone: every citation target is reachable now.

The distractor taxonomy collapsed. Setting proofline_id to the arXiv category made “sibling in the same proofline” mean “same category”, nearly every paper. It reads 100% for both candidates and tells you nothing. A finer grouping would fix it.

A specified cross-check was not built. The design called for grading dag_mined a second way: splitting each paper's citations, mining labels from one half and testing the verdict against the other, as a model-free check on the judge anchor. It was not implemented, because doing it properly means re-deriving the labels and re-seeding. Branch A therefore rests on the judge anchor alone, with no independent corroboration.

9.7% of citation edges were lost in the MAG↔arXiv title join, and the loss is not random: it skews toward older and oddly-formatted records, adding a second layer of incompleteness on top of the real one Branch A studies. A further 1.1% pointed at a version written after the citing paper and are dropped.

Conclusion

All four branches ran, and the ordering the brief proposes survives contact with real data: the label source determines the metric, the metric determines what the loop can gate on, and the judge is an instrument that has to be calibrated before it is believed.

What changed is where the leverage sits. On a synthetic corpus at n = 219 every slice was underpowered and no verdict was resolvable; on 60,000 real cards at n = 1,484 every verdict is decisive and the binding constraint turns out to have been the test set all along. The most consequential single decision in the whole exercise was not which scorer to use. It was which label source to believe, where two free, plausible, internally-consistent options disagree by 0.299 nDCG and point to opposite release decisions.

And the cheapest guarantee is still the one that decides the release. Moving supersession out of the ranker and into the candidate set took one metadata filter, converted 2,621 hard-check failures into zero, and turned a BLOCK into a SHIP. It does not out-score the cross-encoder; the version of this page that said so was measuring a filter that could see the future. A ranking weight can shrink an error class, only a filter can eliminate it, and a filter is only as honest as the date it asks.
