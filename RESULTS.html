Four Branches on arXiv

The scorer-evaluation brief was written against a synthetic corpus with planted ground truth. This is what happens when all four of its branches are run against 60,001 real arXiv cards instead: where nothing is planted, the citation graph is genuinely incomplete, and half the record is superseded.

What was measured

158,160 arXiv papers were joined to the ogbn-arxiv citation graph, then a connected subgraph was grown by snowball sampling: random sampling would have kept only ~(2.5/158)² of the edges and destroyed the graph that makes this corpus worth using. Every paper version becomes its own card, chained by supersedes_id, so supersession is real rather than synthesised.

Embeddings are SPECTER2 (768-dim), which is asymmetric: documents encode through the proximity adapter, queries through adhoc_query. Four candidates were compared against a bm25 baseline. The 30-year span is not incidental: a query dated 1998 is answered against a corpus holding nothing after 1998, so the brief's argument that a frozen test set goes wrong rather than merely stale has three decades of room to bite.

A

Gold from nowhere

Only dag_mined picks the winner both judges picked. The other two sources would have blocked a shipped improvement.

With no planted truth and no humans, the anchor is a calibrated judge scoring the two rankings directly on realistic queries (paper titles) with no labels at all, which is what makes it independent of the sources it grades.

dag_mined and usage_mined differ by 0.218 nDCG on the same corpus, the same two scorers, the same afternoon, and land on opposite sides of the ship/block line. Both are free, both derive from data the product already holds, and both look entirely respectable in isolation. That is the brief's thesis: label quality and label usefulness are different properties, demonstrated rather than argued.

The direction is predictable in hindsight. usage_mined labels are built from what the incumbent surfaced in its own top 3, so a scorer that finds something the incumbent never showed is punished by construction. The brief calls this the source to be genuinely afraid of. It is off by 0.218.

The best label source also disqualifies a whole class of scorer

On this corpus dag_mined relevance is the citation graph, and graph_boost scores partly by in-degree in that same graph, so the comparison would score the scorer on its own feature. The harness refuses it in code rather than trusting anyone to remember:

dag_mined   REFUSED: scorer 'graph_boost' reads graph structure and labels 'dag_mined' are derived from it. The comparison would score the scorer on its own feature.

Against the two sources that could score it, graph_boost is catastrophic: −0.665 on generated, −0.497 on usage_mined; in-degree promotes heavily-cited papers, which is precisely wrong when the task is to find one specific paper. But that is the side note. The finding is that the only label source which picked the right winner is also the one that disqualifies any scorer reading graph structure, while the brief's own recommended stack includes graph-aware retrieval (§6.10). Those two recommendations collide, and the collision is invisible unless the guard is mechanical.

The more reliable judge is the less useful one. Sonnet 5 is markedly more self-consistent and barely position-biased, and agrees with the ranking metric not at all (κ = −0.007). Haiku is noisier and more biased, yet tracks nDCG better. A judge cannot be selected on reliability alone.

The noise floor, and why the brief needs it

Swap-inconsistency is read throughout the brief as position bias. That only holds for a deterministic judge, and ours is not: removing temperature: 0 to satisfy Sonnet 5, which rejects the parameter outright, left every Anthropic judge sampling: two runs over identical inputs returned κ of +0.121 and +0.197.

So the same pair was judged twice in the same order. Any flip there involves no position change at all:

swap-flip rate = noise + position  →  position = swap − floor

Both judges' swap rates survive the correction: position bias is real and dominant. But roughly a sixth of Haiku's raw 25.3% was the judge disagreeing with itself, a different defect with a different fix (sample twice and take the mode). Without this control the two are indistinguishable.

C

The long record

Degradation is monotonic across a 10× corpus, and the candidates' relative advantage grows as the record gets longer.

At a matched operating point, BM25 fabricates an answer for a third of queries whose evidence is genuinely absent from the corpus; both candidates never do. The effect is so large that ~16 no-answer queries would suffice to call it. The brief worries you need several hundred and nobody wants to write them; here the subsetting produced them for free: every no-answer query is the title of a real paper held outside the sampled corpus, so its emptiness is verifiable rather than asserted.

Nothing came back UNDERPOWERED. At n = 1,483 the minimum detectable effect is 0.005–0.023, and 84 to 178 queries, depending on the candidate, would resolve the 0.02 target. The same harness on the synthetic corpus at n = 219 returned UNDERPOWERED on every single slice. That contrast is the brief's sizing argument made concrete: the test set was the binding constraint, not the scorers.

Two details worth trusting. recently_changed is hybrid_rrf_fresh's strongest slice (+0.054, double its overall), precisely where a freshness filter should pay, so the mechanism is working as designed rather than by accident. And the held-out-proofline slice beats the dev split for all three candidates (+0.054 vs +0.037 for rerank), which is the opposite of the leakage pattern the brief warns about.

Which branch binds

Three branches are downstream of one. The ordering A → D → C → B survives every measurement here, and two of its rungs can now be given numbers instead of arguments.

“Build D as a reporter first” is no longer a caution. The identical Branch D machinery, pointed at usage_mined instead of dag_mined, returns WORSE and blocks hybrid_rrf_fresh , the candidate that ships on every hard check and every slice. Not a hypothetical about automating confident wrong answers on a schedule; a specific release decision inverted by a label-source choice, where both sources look respectable.

One place the data pushes back. C sits third as an evaluation branch, and that is right for build order. But its single idea, moving supersession out of the ranker and into the candidate set, was the largest retrieval win measured here and the change that flipped the gate. On the build-order axis C is third; on the “what actually improved retrieval” axis its one insight beat everything else tried, including the cross-encoder.

B last is confirmed on cost as well as value. It consumed real money, three failed runs and several hours of wall clock, and returned two judges agreeing with each other only moderately and the more reliable of them agreeing with the ranking metric not at all. Its one load-bearing use was anchoring Branch A, a service to A rather than a branch in its own right.

What the numbers confirm, and one place they correct

The upstream bundle is worth 2.3× the gap between the two scorers under debate. The brief's caveat holds, and that is a floor, since field-weighted lexical was never tested.

The brief warns that if the goal is better retrieval rather than better evaluation, the largest win is probably not in any of the four branches: it is a cross-encoder reranker, plus the candidate-set stage, plus field-weighted lexical. Decomposed step by step, that is what the data says.

The two scorers whose comparison the whole exercise exists to settle differ by +0.0119, the smallest of the three steps. The upstream work still on the table is worth +0.0274, or 2.30× as much. Three months of measurement would resolve the smaller number.

The one correction is narrower than it first looks. Within that bundle the candidate-set filter (+0.0155) is at least as large as the cross-encoder (+0.0119), so “reranking is probably the single largest available win” does not hold: the cheap metadata filter edges the model. But the two steps are close, and separating them properly needs a paired test on the difference, which was not run.

Three corrections found by running it

  • “Every branch except B1's label comparison runs unchanged on real data” is false. Six sites outside Branch A hardcoded the planted provenance. With real data they evaluate zero queries and report success rather than failing: the branches are downstream of A in code, not only in argument.
  • Swap-inconsistency is not position bias without a noise control, and the brief treats them as one quantity throughout.
  • The scale test and the query set are coupled. B3 pins every judged document into every scale point; real papers cite ~19 others, so a large query set pins more documents than the smallest point holds and the “6k” point silently becomes 30k. Sampling had to run against a judged-document budget, not a query count: the Branch C trap, one level up from where the brief catches it.

What this does not establish

The anchor is a judge, not a human. A judge-anchored Branch A is weaker evidence than a human-anchored one, and the judge grading the label sources has measured biases of its own.

Both judges are from one family. The cross-family comparison the brief asks for was blocked by an exhausted daily quota. Two Anthropic models should agree more than two from different vendors, so κ = 0.462 is an optimistic ceiling, which strengthens rather than weakens the conclusion.

Some hard-check zeros are true by construction. hybrid_rrf_fresh and rerank filter to current versions, so they cannot rank a superseded card above its successor. That is the design working as intended, but it is not a ranking achievement. They are also handicapped: some citation-derived relevance targets are superseded versions, which those scorers can never retrieve.

The distractor taxonomy collapsed. Setting proofline_id to the arXiv category made “sibling in the same proofline” mean “same category”, nearly every paper. It reads 100% for both candidates and tells you nothing. A finer grouping would fix it.

A specified cross-check was not built. The design called for grading dag_mined a second way: splitting each paper's citations, mining labels from one half and testing the verdict against the other, as a model-free check on the judge anchor. It was not implemented, because doing it properly means re-deriving the labels and re-seeding. Branch A therefore rests on the judge anchor alone, with no independent corroboration.

9.7% of citation edges were lost in the MAG↔arXiv title join, and the loss is not random: it skews toward older and oddly-formatted records, adding a second layer of incompleteness on top of the real one Branch A studies.

Conclusion

All four branches ran, and the ordering the brief proposes survives contact with real data: the label source determines the metric, the metric determines what the loop can gate on, and the judge is an instrument that has to be calibrated before it is believed.

What changed is where the leverage sits. On a synthetic corpus at n = 219 every slice was underpowered and no verdict was resolvable; on 60,001 real cards at n = 1,483 every verdict is decisive and the binding constraint turns out to have been the test set all along. The most consequential single decision in the whole exercise was not which scorer to use. It was which label source to believe, where two free, plausible, internally-consistent options disagree by 0.218 nDCG and point to opposite release decisions.

And the cheapest guarantee was worth more than the cleverest model. Moving supersession out of the ranker and into the candidate set took one metadata filter, converted 2,532 hard-check failures into zero, added more nDCG than either the fusion step or the cross-encoder, and turned a BLOCK into a SHIP. It is also the step that makes a hard zero possible at all: a ranking weight can shrink an error class, only a filter can eliminate it.
