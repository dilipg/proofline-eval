Multi-hop Retrieval: DAG vs Ontology

Which way of ingesting a research record finds evidence spread across several documents, and answers as of the right date: the citation DAG in Postgres, walked with SQL, or an ontology extracted with Semantica? On 1,730 planted multi-hop questions the ontology wins, and the reason is how it connects documents, not the database it lives in.

The answer

The ontology beats DAG + SQL on every stratum and on both tests of time. With realistic Haiku extraction the margin is +0.028 chain_recall@10; with perfect extraction it is +0.160.

A question counts only when every hop's evidence is in the top 10 (chain_recall@10). Every comparison is paired on the same questions with a 2,000-sample bootstrap, and a gold-chain oracle row reads 1.000 in both runs, so the scoring itself is sound.

What was measured

The synthetic corpus plants a typed entity layer (methods, datasets, metrics) into card text, then composes questions MuSiQue-style: the question speaks in one card's words and never names the entity that bridges to the answer. Seven families: bridge and bridge3 (the answer is two or three cards away), chrono_asof (the same question either side of a change), timeline, history (every version before a revision), aggregate_set and aggregate_count. Any question a single hop can answer is dropped, with its pair.

Both walks start from the same ten first-stage seeds, rank with the same personalized PageRank (alpha 0.5, 20 iterations) and spend the same top 10. Only the graph differs.

Two extractions feed the ontology. Haiku (claude-haiku-4-5) is the realistic one. Regex reads the corpus's own templates back perfectly, so it is an upper bound on what an ontology can do here, not a realistic ingestion.

The first version of this benchmark could not answer the question: 97% of its evidence chains had no DAG path at all, so the generator chose the winner. Half the methods are now planted inside the DAG (the cards that use and report a method cite the card that introduced it, as real papers do), and every question is stratified by its measured DAG distance: DAG-reachable when all its evidence is within 3 hops along citations that existed at its date, entity-only otherwise.

Why the ontology wins

The DAG reaches the evidence; it cannot rank it. A shared named entity is a one-step bridge between two documents; a shared citation is a two-step bridge through a hub, and everything one step away outranks it.

The trace confirms it. On 40 DAG-reachable bridge questions the SQL walk reached the answer card in 38, then ranked it a median 79th of about 426 cards reached. Reach is not the DAG's problem; the shape of its connection is. A citation says two papers are related. A shared entity says what they share, which is what a multi-hop question asks for.

Extraction quality is the other lever, and the larger one. The same walk over a perfect ontology scores 0.173; over Haiku's, 0.042. Haiku extracted 1,204 entities and 28,041 mentions where the planted layer has about 400 entities, and its mention precision was 0.189 on 24 September: most of what it links is noise, and noise dilutes the bridge. The ontology's advantage over the DAG is real at both qualities, but its size is set by the extractor.

Chronological adherence

Both methods are as-of correct by construction; the ontology is also better at using time. Its fact validity windows are worth 0.034 on questions whose answer changes at a date.

Neither walk can return a card written after the question or a version superseded by then: both answer through the same as-of mask, and the hard checks read zero for both. What can differ is whether a method finds the right version on each side of a change, and the full history of a revision.

The DAG's dates change nothing measurable, because a citation can only point back in time: a date filter almost never removes a path. The ontology's facts connect entities, so a fact recorded after the question can route the walk to the wrong version, and its validity windows stop that.

Chain order does not separate them. The DAG walk returns evidence in near exact reverse time order (Kendall tau −0.84), since citations point to the past; the ontology walk's order is unrelated to time (tau 0.08). Either store can sort by date, so order is metadata, not an ingestion property.

The DAG walk is two Postgres round trips per question (the recursive walk, then the links among what it reached) over about 400 cards. Re-extracting 1,586 cards with Haiku after the corpus changed took 31 minutes with zero errors; the DAG needs no extraction.

What this does not establish

A real corpus. The questions and the entity layer are planted in synthetic text. The real share of questions whose evidence follows citations is unknown; the verdict holds in both strata, so the mix would change its size, not its direction.

The best DAG ranking. The DAG walk is cut at 3 hops and the ontology walk is not, though both use the same seeds, walk and budget. A DAG-native score, such as weighting shared references by how rare they are, was not tried and could close part of the gap.

A good extractor. Regex is an upper bound because it reads the corpus's own templates. Haiku over-extracts made-up vocabulary; on real text it may do better or worse.

Good absolute recall. No method gets every hop on more than 17% of questions, and on three-card bridges the two methods do not differ. This compares two weak multi-hop retrievers.

Abstention. Every method answers the no-answer questions (52 of 57); a ranker always returns ten cards. That is scored with generated answers, not here.

The LLM walker. An LLM-guided ontology walk exists (onto_llm_walk), but its row here ran a scripted stand-in on 81 questions, so it is evidence about the harness only.

Conclusion

For multi-hop questions over a research record, ingest an ontology. It wins where the DAG has no path, and it wins where the DAG does, because a named entity tells the walk what two documents share and a citation only tells it that they are related. It also uses time better: its fact validity windows keep an as-of question on the right version, which the DAG's dates never need to do.

Two cautions travel with that. The advantage is only as large as the extraction is clean: +0.160 with a perfect ontology, +0.028 with Haiku's. And the DAG is not wasted: it is as-of correct for free, it orders evidence in time by its structure, and it needs no extraction bill. The cheapest next step is a DAG ranking built for citations, which would show how much of the gap is the walk rather than the graph.
